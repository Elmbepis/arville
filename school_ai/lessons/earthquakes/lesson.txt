# Earthquakes
Why the ground shakes — and how to stay safe.

## What is an earthquake?
An **earthquake** is a sudden shaking of the ground. It happens when rocks deep inside the Earth break and slip past each other. The energy released travels through the ground as **seismic waves** — and we feel that as shaking.

Earthquakes can last a few seconds or more than a minute. Some are so small you don't feel them at all. Others are strong enough to knock down buildings.

## What causes earthquakes?
The Earth's outer shell is broken into huge slabs of rock called **tectonic plates**. These plates float on the softer rock below and move very slowly — a few centimeters per year — like puzzle pieces drifting on water.

Where two plates meet, they don't always slide smoothly. They can:
- **Push against each other** — building up pressure
- **Pull apart** — creating gaps
- **Slide past each other** — catching and sticking

Eventually, the pressure becomes too much and the rocks **snap**. The sudden movement releases energy as an earthquake.

The point underground where the rocks break is called the **focus**. The point on the surface directly above it is the **epicenter**. Shaking is strongest near the epicenter.

## The Philippines and earthquakes
The Philippines sits along the **Ring of Fire** — a horseshoe-shaped zone around the Pacific Ocean where many earthquakes and volcanoes happen. Because we are on the Ring of Fire, the Philippines experiences **thousands of earthquakes every year**. Most are too small to feel, but a few are strong.

**PHIVOLCS** (Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology) monitors earthquakes across the country. It records every tremor and issues alerts after big ones. It also runs regular earthquake drills in schools and offices.

## How we measure earthquakes

### Magnitude
**Magnitude** tells us how much energy was released. It's measured on the **Richter scale**, where each whole number is about 32 times stronger than the one before.

- **Below 3.0** — usually not felt
- **3.0–3.9** — felt by some people, no damage
- **4.0–4.9** — felt by many, small objects may fall
- **5.0–5.9** — felt strongly, damage to weak buildings
- **6.0–6.9** — strong shaking, damage in populated areas
- **7.0 and above** — major earthquake, serious damage over a wide area

### Intensity
**Intensity** describes how strong the shaking feels at a specific place. The same earthquake has one magnitude but many intensities — stronger near the epicenter, weaker farther away.

## Safety: what to do

### Before an earthquake
- Know your **evacuation route** and where safe open areas are
- Prepare an **emergency kit** — water, food, flashlight, radio, first-aid, whistle
- Practice **duck, cover, and hold** drills
- Learn where the gas valve and main switch are

### During an earthquake
Remember: **Duck, Cover, and Hold**.
1. **Duck** — drop to your hands and knees so you don't fall
2. **Cover** — get under a sturdy table or desk; protect your head and neck
3. **Hold** — hold on to the table until the shaking stops

If you're outside, move to an **open area** away from buildings, trees, and power lines. If you're in a car, pull over to a clear spot and stay inside.

### After an earthquake
- Check yourself and others for injuries
- Expect **aftershocks** — smaller earthquakes that follow the main one
- Use a flashlight, not matches (there may be gas leaks)
- Stay away from damaged buildings
- If you're near the coast and shaking was strong, move to higher ground — a **tsunami** may follow

## Why earthquakes matter
Earthquakes can change landscapes, damage homes and roads, and cause fires or landslides. But understanding them helps us prepare. Buildings in earthquake zones are now designed to sway without collapsing. Warning systems can give people a few seconds of warning before shaking arrives.

## Try this at home
Put your hands flat on a table. Push them toward each other, pressing hard. Now let one hand suddenly slip. That slip is like an earthquake — pressure building up, then releasing as a sudden jerk.
