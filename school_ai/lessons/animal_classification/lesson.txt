# Classifying Animals
How scientists group animals by their features.

## Why classify?
There are millions of different animals on Earth. To study them, scientists group them by things they have in common — like whether they have a backbone, what they eat, or how they have babies. Grouping things by their features is called **classification**.

## The biggest split: vertebrates vs invertebrates

### Vertebrates
**Vertebrates** are animals with a **backbone**. The backbone is a column of small bones that runs down their back and protects their spinal cord.

About 4% of all animals are vertebrates. They include:
- Mammals
- Birds
- Fish
- Reptiles
- Amphibians

### Invertebrates
**Invertebrates** are animals **without a backbone**. They are by far the largest group — about 96% of all animal species.

They include:
- Insects (beetles, ants, butterflies)
- Arachnids (spiders, scorpions)
- Mollusks (snails, octopuses, clams)
- Crustaceans (crabs, shrimp)
- Worms (earthworms, leeches)

## The five vertebrate groups

### Mammals
Fur or hair, feed their babies milk, warm-blooded. Examples: dogs, whales, humans.

### Birds
Feathers, lay eggs, have wings. Most can fly. Warm-blooded. Examples: eagles, chickens, penguins.

### Fish
Live in water, breathe with gills, have scales and fins. Cold-blooded. Examples: tilapia, sharks, goldfish.

### Reptiles
Dry scaly skin, lay eggs on land, cold-blooded. Examples: snakes, lizards, turtles, crocodiles.

### Amphibians
Moist skin, live partly in water and partly on land. Start life with gills, then develop lungs. Cold-blooded. Examples: frogs, toads, salamanders.

## Grouping by what they eat
Animals can also be grouped by their food:
- **Herbivores** — plant eaters (cows, rabbits, caterpillars)
- **Carnivores** — meat eaters (lions, eagles, snakes)
- **Omnivores** — eat both (humans, pigs, chickens)

## Grouping by habitat
- **Terrestrial** — live on land (elephants, lions)
- **Aquatic** — live in water (fish, dolphins)
- **Aerial** — spend most time in the air (birds, bats)

## Why classification matters
Grouping animals helps us understand them. When we know an animal is a mammal, we already know a lot about it — it has fur, feeds its young milk, and is warm-blooded. Classification turns millions of animals into a few understandable groups.
